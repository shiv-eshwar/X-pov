# 12. Q1 WRITING STYLE — ABSOLUTE REQUIREMENT

The manuscript must read like a strong peer-reviewed Q1 journal article.

Writing must be:

- precise;
- technically mature;
- analytical;
- evidence-driven;
- logically structured;
- scientifically cautious;
- grammatically polished;
- terminology-consistent;
- compact without being shallow.

Do not merely generate technically correct sentences.

# THE SENTENCES MUST FLOW INTO ONE ANOTHER.

Each sentence should have a logical relationship with the sentence immediately before it and prepare the reader for the sentence immediately after it.

The writing should feel like **continuous reasoning**, not isolated factual statements.

## EXPLANATION DEPTH CALIBRATION — HARD REQUIREMENT

Allocate explanation according to conceptual difficulty, novelty, and importance rather than according to how easy a topic is to describe.

Do **not** over-explain elementary, standard, or self-evident concepts that the target IEEE Access/Q1 readership can reasonably be expected to know. Avoid textbook-style definitions, extended tutorials, obvious examples, and repeated explanations of simple operations merely to add length.

Conversely, do **not** leave difficult material as unexplained jargon. Complex, specialized, newly introduced, mathematically non-trivial, domain-specific, or method-critical concepts must receive enough explanation for a technically competent reader to understand:

- what the concept means in this study;
- why it is needed;
- how it operates or enters the method;
- which assumptions it relies on;
- how it connects to equations, implementation, or experiments where relevant;
- and what effect it has on the interpretation of the results.

When a sophisticated term first appears, define or contextualize it at the level necessary for the paper rather than merely naming it. When a standard term needs no explanation, use it directly and move on.

The intended balance is:

**simple/standard concept → concise treatment**

**complex/novel/critical concept → proportionately deeper treatment**

Never spend a paragraph explaining something elementary while leaving the actual difficult mechanism, formulation, assumption, or inference unexplained.

# 19. ABSTRACT — MOTIVATION + GAP + WORK + EXPERIMENTS + FINDINGS

# CRITICAL REQUIREMENT

The Abstract must NOT read like a list of tasks performed.

Do not begin immediately with:

"This paper proposes..."

unless the scientific context has first been established sufficiently.

The Abstract must balance:

# **WHY THE STUDY IS NEEDED**

with:

# **WHAT THE STUDY ACTUALLY DID**

and:

# **WHAT THE EXPERIMENTS ACTUALLY FOUND**

The abstract should follow a natural connected progression:

### 1. SCIENTIFIC NEED / CONTEXT

Briefly establish the domain-specific practical or scientific problem.

Explain why the problem matters.

This should be specific rather than generic.

### 2. CURRENT LIMITATION / RESEARCH GAP

Explain what existing approaches fail to address and why the limitation is consequential.

### 3. PRESENT STUDY / CONTRIBUTION

Introduce what this study develops, modifies, models, or investigates.

### 4. EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION

Briefly state:

- the real dataset/environment;
- major experimental protocol;
- principal comparisons;
- validation strategy.

### 5. CORE QUANTITATIVE FINDINGS

Report the strongest genuine experimental findings.

### 6. SCIENTIFIC SIGNIFICANCE

Conclude with what the findings mean rather than simply repeating performance numbers.

A useful conceptual balance is approximately:

**25–35% motivation/problem/gap**

and

**65–75% contribution/method/experiments/findings/significance**

without treating these as rigid word quotas.

The Abstract must read as one coherent scientific argument.

Every sentence should logically lead to the next.

Avoid writing:

"We propose X. We use dataset Y. We achieve Z. We outperform A."

as four disconnected statements.

Instead connect:

**problem → limitation → methodological response → experimental evidence → implication**

The Abstract must contain **both why the research was necessary and what was actually accomplished**.

Do not include fabricated or expected Results.

Use only evidence already obtained from executed experiments.
